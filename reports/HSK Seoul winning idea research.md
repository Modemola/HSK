# Corridor wins by making HSP compliance visible

The single most winnable idea for HSK Chain Horizon Seoul is a synthesis we call **Corridor**. Licensed remitters' AI agents settle Korea-to-Hong Kong stablecoin transfers through **HSP's compliant payment mode**, which needs `attests:kyc` and `attests:sanctions` and is checked independently with `HSPVerifier`. Real USDC.e is escrowed and released on **HashKey Chain mainnet**. Every transfer automatically produces a Travel-Rule packet and an FX-report evidence record. It fits the competitive gap exactly. Of roughly 40 public Seoul repos, **none uses HSP's compliant payments, attestations, verifier, x402 facilitator or custom adapters**, and **none targets remitters, merchants or institutions**. Track 1 has become a monoculture of about 20 "guarded vault" trading agents, several of them already live on mainnet. Corridor also mirrors HashKey's own planned **Korea–Hong Kong remittance PoC with KYC/AML** under its July 2026 Kbank MOU. It lands on the day Korea's FX Act amendment on cross-border virtual-asset transfers reportedly takes effect, **3 Dec 2026**, the hackathon deadline itself. It follows the pattern past HashKey winners share: an agent that moves real stablecoins, compliance treated as a feature, an honest real-versus-mocked table, and mainnet settlement. The main uncertainty is operational. The HSP sandbox is documented only on testnet, and nobody has confirmed whether the compliant scenario and its KYC/sanctions issuer are actually live for Seoul teams. Ask the organizers on day one. The backup is an attested RWA subscription agent (delivery versus payment, or DvP) that reuses about 70% of Corridor's engine.

## Two crowded templates and one empty lane in the Seoul field

The visible Seoul competition sorts into two templates. A GitHub sweep of about 105 READMEs found **about 26 Track 1 repos and about 15 Track 2 repos**. More than 80% are either a "guarded trading vault" (an LLM or rule agent plus an owner vault with caps, a kill switch and a decision log) or an "EIP-712 mandate + escrow + receipt" A2A job market ([competitor sweep, e.g. Leash](https://github.com/SuperPhant/leash-hsk); [mandatesettle](https://github.com/phee24/mandatesettle)). The top of Track 1 is already strong and live. **Leash, Keel, Glassbox and SayTrade all run verified mainnet vaults** with real trades, and the "prove-yourself" variants (Glassbox's Brier-scored predictions, Kharon's provable refusals) have taken the novelty that remained in that template ([Glassbox](https://github.com/Tharun2075/glassbox); [Kharon](https://github.com/rmndkyl/hsk-guardrail)). Kharon also measured that the HSKSwap WHSK/USDT pool tracks the world price, so a directional agent has no edge to show.

Track 2 is thinner at the top. Only **caremandate, Tandem, mandatesettle and Tharun's leash** make credible HSP calls, and all four use the "HSP Agentic Payment" MCP gateway (`create_order` / `prepare_payment` / `submit_payment`); Tharun's defaults to mock ([caremandate](https://github.com/swiftbotlabs/caremandate); [Tandem](https://github.com/k58v2hbzkk-del/Tandem)). Caremandate is the strongest Korea-localised rival. It pays a carer in Busan for each confirmed visit, encodes a won-to-USDC rate, and comes from the team behind India's hsk-meter, which already shipped a **KYC-SBT compliant mode** ([hsk-meter](https://github.com/swiftbotlabs/hsk-meter)). That team is the one most likely to move into compliance in Seoul.

The empty lane appears when the field is set against the official HSP guide. The guide makes four scenarios first-class: public evm-transfer, public x402, **compliant evm-transfer** and **compliant x402**. A verifier ACCEPTs only if `requiredCapabilities ⊆ satisfiedCapabilities`. The guide also offers `@hsp/devkit` for custom adapters with a conformance suite ([HSP developer guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/HashkeyHSK/documentation/HEAD/contents/docs/HSP/index.mdx)). A keyword scan found **zero Seoul entries using attestations, compliant payments, the HSP x402 facilitator or a custom adapter**, and zero hits for remittance, corridor or cross-border. The nearest precedent is April's ZKRemit, from a different edition ([ZKRemit](https://github.com/Kodark220/ZKRemit)). The user segments are also unclaimed: nearly every entry targets a "retail bot owner" or a "generic agent developer", and none targets merchants, licensed VASPs, remitters or LPs.

Two caveats limit this map. It only covers public repos. DoraHacks was unreachable, and at least one Mumbai team kept its repo "private until submit" ([agentsettle-hsk](https://github.com/0xultravioleta/agentsettle-hsk)). The quality ratings also rest on README claims, not on running code.

Past HashKey podiums tell a consistent story. The global Horizon edition (Apr 2026) gave **PayFi 1st to FlowLink** and **2nd to HSP Payment Agent** ([DoraHacks report](https://dorahacks.io/hackathon/2045/report)). FlowLink calls itself an "agent-native compliance-first payment layer" with OFAC screening, velocity limits and signed receipts ([FlowLink](https://github.com/Akasxh/flowlink)). The Japan edition (Jul 2026) gave **DeFi 1st to AllScale Agentic Commerce Gateway** ([HSK Chain on X](https://x.com/HSKChain/status/2077272198910529876?lang=en)). AllScale is an agent checkout gateway **deployed on chain 177 against real bridged USDC.e**. Its README frankly labels its protocol adapters, KYT and zkTLS gates as mocks ([AllScale](https://github.com/slucifersz/allscale-agentic-commerce-gateway)). An agent-payments project winning a "DeFi" track shows that judges reward settlement infrastructure whatever the track label. Japan's stated criteria were **real-world demand, commercialization potential, UX and potential to become on-chain financial infrastructure** ([HackathonRadar](https://www.hackathonradar.com/database/hackathon/hashkey-chain-horizon-hackathon-japan-demo-day-2026)). Mumbai, whose tracks matched Seoul's, used **five judges: three ecosystem partners and two from HashKey Chain**, and required HSP for the payments track ([Luma Mumbai](https://luma.com/hashkey-demo-day-mumbai)). No judge names or quotes were found for any edition, and the Mumbai winners could not be retrieved. Both gaps are real.

The wider 2026 hackathon scene points the same way. Consensus Miami was won by a boring pay-per-call commodity-price API settled on mainnet ([CoinDesk](https://www.coindesk.com/tech/2026/05/08/ai-agents-fueled-a-frenzy-of-startup-building-at-the-consensus-miami-easya-hackathon)). Colosseum Frontier gave a payments win to **DashX, compliant cross-border stablecoin payments for emerging markets** ([Colosseum](https://blog.colosseum.com/announcing-the-winners-of-the-solana-frontier-hackathon/)). Trading hackathons rewarded agents whose AI "never pulls the trigger" unchecked ([lablab](https://x.com/lablabai/status/2105583053867495711)). The industry's unsolved problems sit after payment: refunds on irreversible push payments, dispute evidence, statements and reconciliation, and the fact that "authorization does not prove intent" ([fraudbeat](https://www.fraudbeat.com/agentic-commerce-disputes-friendly-fraud/); [a16z on stablecoin gaps](https://blockchain.news/news/a16z-stablecoins-ai-agent-b2b-payments)).

HashKey's 2026 strategy points at the same target. HashKey sells itself as the licensed, listed, compliance-first group ([The Block](https://www.theblock.co/post/382902/hashkey-hong-kong-debut)). It describes HSP as producing "**auditable, compliant financial credentials**" for agent payments ([EIN Presswire](https://www.einpresswire.com/article/956165566/hashkey-hsp-expands-offline-retail-footprint-via-on-chain-settlement-integration-with-pandabot-caf)). HSP's commercial deals are cross-border commerce (ONERWAY) and offline retail (Pandabot), not agent-to-agent micropayments ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/hashkey-settlement-platform-hsp-and-onerway-announce-strategic-partnership-to-shape-the-future-of-on-chain-payments-and-support-cross-border-commerce-development-302868883.html)). Citi estimates agentic payments could add **about HK$1.59bn of annual revenue by 2030** ([HashKey/Citi](https://group.hashkey.com/en/citi-initiates-coverage-on-hashkey-with-buy-rating-hk5-6-target-price/)). The Korea link is explicit. The **HashKey–Kbank–BPMG MOU (21 Jul 2026) plans a Korea–Hong Kong blockchain remittance PoC with KYC/AML** ([HashKey newsroom](https://group.hashkey.com/en/newsroom/hashkey-signs-strategic-mou-with-kbank-and-bpmg-to-strengthen-asia-connect-ecosystem)). The Seoul event is organised by pentabase ([HSK Chain on X](https://x.com/HSKChain)).

Regulation sharpens the pitch and also sets traps. Korea's **FX Transactions Act amendment (passed 7 May 2026)** creates a registered "virtual-asset transfer service" category with reporting through the Bank of Korea. Its effective date is reported as **3 Dec 2026**, though no official confirmation was found ([BKL](https://www.bkl.co.kr/en/law/insight/newsletter/6819); [crypto.news](https://crypto.news/south-korea-crypto-transfer-rules-december-2026/)). Hwanchigi, illegal FX through crypto, accounts for about **US$6.4bn of US$7.1bn** in customs crypto-crime referrals, with USDT as the preferred tool ([Crystal Intelligence](https://crystalintelligence.com/investigations/inside-south-koreas-7-1b-crypto-crime-problem/)). KRW stablecoins are still not legal to issue, and the draft Digital Asset Basic Act puts them behind bank-majority consortia ([Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/digital-assets/2026/11/16/south-korea-will-let-tech-platforms-lead-won-stablecoins-from-behind-a-bank-majority-while-taiwan-wants-one-bank-in-charge/)). Hong Kong's Travel Rule applies regardless of amount, with full originator and beneficiary data required at **HK$8,000 or more** ([Notabene](https://notabene.id/world/hong-kong)). The practical rules follow from this. Do not mint a KRW coin. Do not use USDT as the payment rail. Never pitch peer-to-peer KRW conversion. Present the product as **infrastructure for licensed operators**, not as a remittance business.

It is worth more as Corridor's engine than as the product. **Tourist payments** have large demand: a record **US$14.1B in foreign card spend** in 2025 ([Korea JoongAng Daily](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/business/foreign-visitor-card-spending-in-korea-climbs-to-record-high/12856039)). But tourists already pay by card. Bank and card pilots (BC Card/Coinbase, Hana/Circle) own the use case ([icobench](https://icobench.com/news/bc-card-completes-stablecoin-payment-pilot-for-foreign-tourists-in-south-korea/)), and one merchant call settles the payment, so no second agent is needed.

The original **Corridor** ranks just below the synthesis for two reasons. Its reverse auction invites the question "why not a quote API?". And its KR→Vietnam/Nepal retail framing sits closer to hwanchigi optics on the week the FX rules change. Its demand case is still the best documented: **USD 17.63B** sent home by migrant workers over five years, about **US$3.5B a year** ([The Fact](https://news.tf.co.kr/read/economy/2252761.htm)), against a global average cost of **6.49%** for US$200 and a G20 target of 3% ([World Bank RPW](https://remittanceprices.worldbank.org)). The synthesis keeps that market as the expansion story. It moves the demo corridor to KR→HK, which HashKey has itself committed to prototype, and replaces the auction with quote-and-escrow, compliance-preserving refunds and an evidence pack. Those additions target the post-payment gaps the industry names as unsolved.

## The pick: Corridor, compliance rails for Korea's new transfer regime

**One-line pitch.** *Corridor lets licensed remitters' AI agents settle Korea→Hong Kong stablecoin transfers through HSP compliant payments, so every transfer carries verified KYC and sanctions attestations, a Travel-Rule packet and an FX-report record the moment it settles.*

**Users and buyer.** The paying customer is a **licensed cross-border operator**: a Korean small-sum remitter or one of the new FX-Act "virtual-asset transfer service" registrants, such as SentBe-type services ([Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/amp/business/companies/20250916/korean-startups-expand-overseas-remittance-services-for-foreign-residents)). The sharpest named buyer is the **Kbank–HashKey–BPMG KR↔HK PoC**, which needs exactly this KYC/AML-bound settlement layer. Senders are the operator's KYC'd customers: SMEs paying Hong Kong suppliers first, migrant workers on later corridors. A **compliance officer or auditor** reads the evidence console. Submit to **Track 2**. Do not bolt on an HSKSwap leg: the only liquid pool is WHSK/USDT, and USDT is the wrong rail to show a Korean panel.

**End-to-end flow.** A KYC'd sender asks the operator to send, for example, ₩3,000,000 to a Hong Kong supplier. The operator's **Sender Agent** turns the request into an AP2-shaped mandate with an amount cap, expiry, payee allowlist and purpose code. It then requests quotes from two or three **Payout Agents** listed in a mainnet `CorridorRegistry`. It picks a quote by fee, ETA and attestation status. It pays through **HSP compliant evm-transfer** with a policy that requires `attests:kyc` and `attests:sanctions`. The winning Payout Agent runs **`HSPVerifier` itself as relying party** and ACCEPTs only if the required capabilities are satisfied. A rival Payout Agent without a sanctions attestation is visibly REJECTed. In parallel, the Sender Agent locks real **USDC.e** in a mainnet `CorridorEscrow`, keyed to the quote ID and the HSP receipt hash. The Payout Agent posts a signed payout confirmation, which releases the escrow. If the SLA lapses, the escrow **auto-refunds the sender and keeps the original attestations attached**, giving the compliance-preserving refund that x402 lacks.

The AI work happens in a **Compliance Agent**. It classifies the purpose of payment, detects **structuring** (for example three HK$7,900 transfers designed to stay under the HK$8,000 Travel-Rule threshold), and refuses or aggregates them. It then assembles an **evidence pack**: mandate, quote, HSP receipt, attestation digests, a Travel-Rule originator/beneficiary packet and a modelled BOK FX-report record. The pack's hash is anchored on mainnet and shown in an auditor console. That "the agent catches the hwanchigi pattern" moment is the demo's emotional peak and the reason this has to be an agent rather than a form.

**What runs where.** The HSP sandbox is documented only on `hashkey-testnet`, with faucet USDC, and no chain-177 Coordinator is documented ([HashFans HSP mirror](https://hashfans.io/docs/HSP)). The design is therefore an openly labelled hybrid.

## What could sink Corridor, and what to ask on day one

The largest risk is **availability of the compliant scenario**. Every Seoul team with a real integration uses the MCP gateway, not the Coordinator SDK ([Tandem](https://github.com/k58v2hbzkk-del/Tandem)). It is therefore unconfirmed whether the Seoul sandbox exposes `attests:kyc`/`attests:sanctions` issuers for arbitrary test addresses. If it does not, Corridor loses its core differentiator. The fallback keeps the architecture: pay via the MCP gateway and use an on-chain `AttestationRegistry` with a clearly labelled mock issuer, which weakens the "deepest HSP use" score from 5 to about 3. Second, **regulatory optics**: a Korean panel will react badly to anything that looks like unlicensed KRW outflow, so the "for licensees, no P2P conversion, USDC.e not USDT" framing has to be said out loud. Third, the **mainnet/testnet split** can read as two disconnected demos unless the receipt-hash anchoring is shown explicitly. Fourth, **competition**: the caremandate/hsk-meter team has shipped KYC-mode HSP before and is the most likely to compete in this lane, and private DoraHacks submissions are invisible. Fifth, **"why agents?"** remains the soft spot of any remittance pitch. The structuring detector and multi-agent quote/verify loop answer it, but only if the demo shows the agent making a decision a rule table could not. Finally, the scores above are judgments built on README-level evidence and search snippets. No Seoul judge, official rubric weight or Mumbai winner was verified.

**Backup pick: an attested RWA subscription agent.** An investor's agent subscribes to a tokenised fund or deposit on chain 177. The issuer's agent mints or transfers an ERC-3643-style token **only after `HSPVerifier` ACCEPTs a compliant payment**, mapping `attests:kyc`/`attests:sanctions` onto the token's identity claims. Redemption runs as the reverse DvP. It reuses Corridor's compliant-payment, verifier, relayer and anchoring code. It also aligns tightly with HashKey's RWA push: on-chain RWA TVL of **HK$2,678.5m, up 167.8% YoY**, and a one-stop issuance platform ([PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/apac/news-releases/hashkey-announces-2026-interim-results-302861713.html); [Cryptonomist](https://en.cryptonomist.ch/2026/02/24/hashkey-tokenisation-rwa-hong-kong/)). Switch to it if day-one answers show the panel leaning toward HK institutional finance over a Korea corridor, or if regulatory sensitivity around cross-border flows proves higher than expected. Its weakness is that no real fund participates, so it reads more like plumbing than a product.

## Conclusion

The field has turned the brief's apparent "hard" track into the open one. Track 1's guarded-vault agents compete on polish against mainnet-verified rivals, in a pool with too little liquidity to show skill. Track 2's HSP compliance layer, the feature that makes HSP different from generic x402, is unused by every visible Seoul entry. The winning move is therefore not a cleverer agent but **the first entry to make HashKey's compliance claim executable**: an attestation-gated payment that a counterparty verifies, real USDC.e on mainnet, and a regulator-shaped evidence trail, on the exact corridor and date HashKey and Korean regulators care about.

Corridor's chance of winning depends less on code than on one answer from the organizers about the compliant sandbox. Ask that question before writing anything else. If the answer is yes, the remaining work is a disciplined seven-day build. If it is no, the same engine moves to the RWA DvP backup with little rework.
